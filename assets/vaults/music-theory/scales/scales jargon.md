You have the major recipe from [[what a scale is]] and the natural minor recipe from [[natural minor scale]]; now the rule that decides what each note gets called.

A scale uses one letter per note, A to G, in order, no repeats. That's not a suggestion. It's why F♯ major has E♯ as its seventh note and not F: F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ E♯. E♯ sounds exactly like F, and it's still the right name, because the letter F has already been used up by F♯ at the start. Letters go in order; accidentals sort out the step sizes.

Sometimes one sharp isn't enough. A **double sharp** (written 𝄪) raises a note by two half steps, one whole step, so F𝄪 sounds like G and still counts as an F. This is the one place in this chapter where double sharps are explained; every other page links back here.

Try it: open the player on [[Scales]], pick G♯ harmonic minor, press Play and watch the last note turn into F double sharp. (Harmonic minor shows up on [[modes and minor scales]].)

## Words you will meet

- **Tonic**: the home note a scale starts and ends on.
- **Degree**: a note's position in the scale, counted from the tonic: 1st, 2nd, 3rd and so on.
- **Octave**: the same note again, 12 half steps up; see [[octave]].
- **Whole and half step**: a half step is the next piano key, black keys count, or the next fret; a whole step is two of them.
- **Accidental**: a sharp, flat or natural sign that changes a note; see [[accidental]].
- **Key signature**: the sharps or flats written once at the start of the staff so you don't repeat them. The circle of fifths comes later.

> [!note]- Structural formula
> F♯ major by letter: F G A B C D E, one letter each, in order.
> Step sizes (W-W-H-W-W-W-H) then fix each accidental: F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ E♯ F♯.
> A double sharp adds 2 half steps to the natural letter.
