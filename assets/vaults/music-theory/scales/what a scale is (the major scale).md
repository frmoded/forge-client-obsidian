Intervals gave you distances; a scale lines a few of them up in a fixed order and turns them into a sound.

A scale is a recipe of steps from a home note, the **tonic**. Change the home note and the recipe stays. That's the whole idea.

## Major

Major is the one everybody knows, the "do re mi" scale. Whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half: W-W-H-W-W-W-H. A half step is the next piano key, black keys included; a whole step is two. Seven steps, and the last one lands back on the tonic, an octave up.

W and H look like code? They're not: [[interval notation and jargon]] explains them.

Build it from C and you never touch a black key. W to D, W to E, H to F, W to G, W to A, W to B, H to C: C D E F G A B C.

![[scales/resources/images/c_major_scale.svg]]
*C major: the white keys from C to C, no sharps or flats.*

Now start on G, same recipe: W to A, W to B, H to C, W to D, W to E, W to F♯, H back to G. The white keys fail you at the seventh step: F is a whole step under G, and the recipe wants a half. So you take the black key and spell it F♯, not G♭, because a scale uses each letter once and G is already home. [[scales jargon]] has the rule. The notes moved. The shape didn't.

![[scales/resources/images/g_major_scale.svg]]
*G major: the same recipe from G; the seventh note is F-sharp, shown here as a sharp sign on the staff.*

Major is the parent scale. Later keys and chords get measured against it.

## Sharp or flat?

The rule that settles every spelling fight: a scale uses each letter from A to G once, in order. Call G major's seventh note G♭ and you've got two Gs and no F. So it's F♯.

That's why a key signature is all sharps or all flats, never a mix.

G major has one sharp, the F♯ above. F major has one flat, and it's the fourth note: B♭, not A♯, because the letters must run F G A B C D E.

Keys a fifth up from C (G, D, A, E, B) collect sharps; keys a fourth up (F, B♭, E♭, A♭) collect flats. Some keys have two spellings: D♭ major and C♯ major sound identical, and the usual pick is fewer sharps or flats, so D♭ (five flats) beats C♯ (seven sharps). A later chapter shows why the sharp keys arrive in the order they do.

![[scales/resources/images/f_major_scale.svg]]
*F major: the same recipe from F; the fourth note is B-flat, shown as a flat sign on the staff.*

## Time to work

Two checks. Count the half steps against the recipe, then read the letters: seven notes, seven different letters, none twice, none skipped. G A B C D E F♯ passes. G A B C D E G♭ fails: two Gs, no F.

Don't stop at C and G. Build all twelve majors in the exercise below; it counts how many you've finished.
