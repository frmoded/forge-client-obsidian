Intervals gave you distances; a scale lines a handful of them up in a fixed order and turns the result into a sound.

A scale is a recipe of steps that starts on a home note, the **tonic**. Change the home note and the recipe stays. That's the whole idea.

The major scale is the one everybody knows: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half, or W-W-H-W-W-W-H. A half step is the next piano key (black keys count) or the next fret; a whole step is two of them. It's the sound people call "do re mi".

W and H look like code? They're not. [[interval notation and jargon]] explains whole steps, half steps and the letters we use for them.

Build it from C and you never touch a black key: C D E F G A B C. Now start on G and follow the same recipe. The white keys let you down at the seventh step, and you need F♯ to keep the shape. Start on F and the fourth note has to be B♭. The notes moved. The shape didn't.

Major is the parent scale. Later keys and chords get measured against it, so get friendly.

Try it: pick a different tonic in the player, leave the scale on major, and watch the same pattern slide to a new home.
