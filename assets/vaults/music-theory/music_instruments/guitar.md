Standard tuning sets the six open strings to **E2 A2 D3 G3 B3 E4** — the same pitch classes ([[pitch_class]]) as any other instrument, just fixed as starting points a few [[semitone|semitones]] apart (four, between the G and B strings; five, everywhere else). Moving up one fret raises a string by exactly one semitone, so once you know where a note is on one string, the rest follow from counting.

Notice the same [[pitch_class]] shows up in several places at once — e.g. the open 2nd string (B3) and fret 4 of the 3rd string are both B, just reached differently. That's the thing a guitar has that a piano doesn't: [[piano|a piano has exactly one key per pitch class per octave]]; a guitar reaches most pitch classes on more than one string.

## Tuning

Between neighbouring strings the gaps, in semitones (half steps), are 5, 5, 5, 4, 5: E to A, A to D, D to G, then G to B, then B to E. Four of the gaps are a perfect fourth, and the G–B gap, one semitone smaller, is a major third. Together that is 24 semitones, exactly two octaves from the low E2 to the high E4.

Why so? A hand covers about four frets comfortably, and a fourth is five semitones, so each string reaches just past the open note of its neighbour: the fifth fret of one string sounds the next string's open note (the fourth fret, for G to B), which is how guitarists tune by ear. Fourths spread six strings across two octaves while keeping everything within reach of one hand, and the single major third makes ordinary chords, built from thirds and fifths, fit under the fingers in compact shapes. Some players use all fourths instead, and pay for it with wider stretches in those chords.

## Two differences from the piano

First, a piano key is a fixed pitch: one key per pitch per octave. A guitarist chooses a pitch by stopping a string, so most pitches can be played in several places. Second, the piano lets each finger play any key independently, while a guitar has six strings and one stopping hand, so at most six notes can sound at once, one per string.

That limit has a sharper edge: some notes can never sound together. A string sounds one pitch at a time, because the fretting finger blocks the string beyond it, so two notes that exist only on the same string cannot be played at once. Low E2 and F2, for example, are both on the lowest string only (open and first fret), so no guitarist can play them together. Fingers also block neighbouring strings, and a hand spans only about four frets, which rules out more combinations. On a piano these are simply two keys.

## History

The word "guitar" comes from the Spanish *guitarra*. By 1200 Spain had two instruments with that name, the *guitarra latina* and the *guitarra morisca*, and the Spanish vihuela of the 15th and 16th centuries was a major influence. The five-course baroque guitar appeared in mid-16th-century Spain and stayed popular in Spain, Italy and France into the mid-18th century. Placing the frets was a tuning problem from the start — see [[the_problem_with_frets]] and the [[lute]].

Around 1850 Spanish makers, notably Antonio de Torres, settled the modern form; Torres enlarged the body and introduced fan bracing, which made the guitar much louder. The electric guitar followed: the first were patented in 1937, and solid-body designs came from Gibson (with Les Paul, whose experiments began with the [[railroad_guitar]]) and Leo Fender after the Second World War.
