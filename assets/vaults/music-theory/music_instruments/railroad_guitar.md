The **Railroad Guitar** — Les Paul called it simply "the Rail" — was his first experiment in a guitar that does not fight its own body: a piece of steel railroad rail about two feet long, with a single guitar string stretched along it. Railroad spikes served as the nut and the bridge, and a telephone microphone under the string, wired into his mother's radio, was the amplifier. The rail is so heavy and stiff that only the string moves, so there is no body resonance, no feedback, and a note that rings almost indefinitely.

Look at what that setup is: one string, two fixed ends, a solid body underneath — a [[monochord]]. Les Paul rebuilt the oldest instrument in music theory out of a piece of railway. The goal is the opposite, though: a monochord's body is there to sound, the Rail's body is there to stay silent, so that the string alone, through its microphone, makes the sound. The string still obeys the same rules of length, tension and mass as in [[frequency]]. Try it: on the monochord you slide the bridge to change the length; on the Rail the spikes are fixed, so how would you change the pitch?

## History

As a boy in Waukesha, Wisconsin, Les Paul built the Rail from a scrap of rail found on the tracks across the street from his house. Thrilled, he ran to tell his mother. She was unimpressed; the story goes that she said, "That'll be the day you see a cowboy on a horse playing a piece of railroad track!" A railway was not a practical guitar.

He kept the idea and traded the steel for wood. Around 1941, working Sundays at the Epiphone factory in New York, he mounted an Epiphone neck, homemade pickups, a bridge and a Vibrola tailpiece on a four-by-four block of pine: **the Log**. It sustained beautifully, but audiences ignored it until he attached the two halves of a sawed-up Epiphone hollow body as "wings", so it looked like a guitar — his conclusion was that people hear with their eyes. Gibson thought it too odd to sell; the Gibson Les Paul solid body arrived in 1952.
