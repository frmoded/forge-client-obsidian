A **step sequencer** turns a bar of music into a grid: time runs left to right in fixed slots, and each slot is either on or off for a given sound. The grid below quantizes each beat into four 16th notes — the standard resolution for a drum pattern, fine enough for most grooves without needing to place a note "between the lines." Sixteen steps make one 4/4 bar; the thicker dividers mark where each beat starts, the same "landmark, not every line" idea as [[note_value|beat subdivisions]] in standard notation.

Program a pattern by clicking steps for kick, snare and hi-hat, then press Play. Mute (M) and Solo (S) isolate a channel; Swing delays every off-beat 16th for a looser feel; four pattern banks (A–D) let you switch between variations without stopping playback — useful for building up a beat section by section, the same way a real drum machine's pattern-chaining works.

**Edit a pattern that lives in a note.** The rhythm data notes in `rhythm_data/` are just JSON, and you can drive them from the grid instead of hand-typing booleans like an animal. Open one — from the explorer, a link, anywhere — and it opens *as* this widget, loaded with the note's pattern. Click cells, change the tempo, swing or time signature, and it saves itself a moment after you stop: the little status next to the note's name says *Saving…* then *Saved*, and there's no Save button to forget. Play, mute, solo and volume never touch the note. **Open as JSON** in the tab's header flips it to the plain text and keeps it there until you navigate away; **Open as Beat Box** flips it back (so does *Edit rhythm in Rhythm Box* in the command palette). A note holds exactly one pattern, so there are no banks in here: want a variation, make another note — that's what the graph is for. Changing the time signature (or removing an instrument that has hits) asks first — a small bar appears right in the widget — because it rewrites the whole pattern in the note. Made a mess? *Restore to last commit* in the same header puts the note back.

**Three views of one note.** The tab of a rhythm note has three modes, and the header buttons flip between them: **Beat Box** (the grid, the default), **JSON** (the plain text, for the hand-editors) and **Score** — the same bar engraved as notation, one staff per instrument, accents as `>`, with a play button. The Score is a *view*: nothing is converted, no note is created, and it re-draws itself when the note changes. It needs the music edition of the plugin; on the lean one you get Beat Box and JSON. To change a hit, go back to the Beat Box — the score is for reading, not for fiddling.

**Beyond the kit.** The grid isn't limited to kick, snare and hi-hat anymore: **Add instrument** brings in a bell, claves, a high conga and a low conga, and a rhythm note shows exactly one row for each instrument it contains — so a bell pattern is a single row, honest and uncluttered. The meter menu has grown to match: 4/4, 3/4, **12/8**, **6/8** and **5/4**, with the steps grouped the way the meter feels (threes in 12/8 and 6/8, fours in 5/4). Open `rhythm_pattern_bell_12_8` for the West African bell, `rhythm_pattern_clave_son_3_2` for the clave that half of Latin music hangs on, `rhythm_pattern_tresillo` for the 3+3+2 cell, `rhythm_pattern_hemiola` for two-against-three, and `rhythm_pattern_tha_dhi_gi_na_thom` for a Carnatic five-beat phrase played on hand drums — a stylised kit rendering, the drums standing in for the syllables. Same grid, very different worlds.

**Grid or circle.** The **Grid | Circle** switch beside the transport redraws the same bar as a circle: step 1 at the top, time running clockwise, one ring per instrument (the first one outermost). Click a dot to toggle a hit, exactly as in the grid. **One toolbar** sits above both views, and its controls are the same, in the same order, whichever view you are in: the **Add instrument** menu first (a bell, claves, a high conga or a low conga — in the circle it arrives as another ring), then **‹ ›** shift *every* instrument one step earlier or later, **⇆ Mirror** plays the bar backwards — the last step becomes the first, every instrument at once, with each hit keeping its loudness, so the son clave's 3-2 turns into its reverse, and pressing Mirror a second time puts it back exactly — and **↺ ↻** turn only the *selected* instrument, counter-clockwise (one step earlier) or clockwise (one step later), leaving the others exactly as they are. To select an instrument, click its name in the grid, or its chip in the circle's toolbar; the highlighted one is selected, and it stays selected when you flip between the views. In the circle the toolbar carries three extras after those: **↑ ↓** move the selected ring outward or inward — the ring order is the order of the instruments in the note, so a move rewrites that order in the note — and *only* in the note: the Score always keeps its fixed instrument order (kick, snare, hi-hat, then the world percussion), so rhythms you chain together never split into extra staves — and **△**, which joins one ring's hits into a polygon, the quickest way to *see* why a rhythm feels the way it does. Hover any of them for its name. The circle is only as big as its rings need, so both views leave more of the page to the music. (There is no Random, Clear, Export or tap-tempo button: the note *is* the pattern — start a new note for a new beat, and set the tempo with **− +**. To make one: **New Forge note**, Note type **Data**, Content type **Rhythm (Beat Box)** — it starts as a plain kick-and-snare groove and opens right here, where your first click already saves.) Nothing about the note changes except the hits themselves; the view is only how you look at them. Up to four instruments fit legibly — at four the Add menu greys out in the circle and says so; with more, the circle says so and the grid stays. The meter menu also offers **4/4 (eighth notes)**: one bar in eight steps instead of sixteen, for patterns that never need the in-between 16ths.

**Put a rhythm in your own note.** Don't copy the pattern and don't paste a second widget — point at the data note and let it play right where you're writing. Drop a `forge-rhythm` block into any note, with a link to a rhythm data note on its first line:

````
```forge-rhythm
[[rhythm_pattern_tresillo]]
```
````

That's the whole thing: the rhythm shows up as a Beat Box in place, headed with the note's name, with **Open note** beside it. Three optional lines can follow the link — `height: 480` (pixels), `title: Three-three-two` (the heading) and `view: circle` (start in the circle view; `view: grid` is the default) — and nothing else; a typo gets you a plain message, not a silent shrug. Without a `height:` line the block **fits itself to the rhythm**: it grows when you add an instrument or switch to the circle, shrinks when you take one away, and never shows a scrollbar of its own. Write `height: 480` and you get exactly 480 pixels, as written.

It's a playground, on purpose. Click cells, shove the tempo, change the meter — nothing is saved, ever. The small *not saved* beside the buttons appears as soon as the embed differs from the note (hover it for the whole sentence) and goes again when it matches — after **Reset**, after an "Updated from note" reload, or when you undo your own change; **Reset** is greyed out until there is something to reset. The buttons share one row with the note's name: **Open note** opens the real thing in its own tab, where changes *are* saved; **Score** (music edition) engraves what is on screen right now; **Reset** puts the rhythm back exactly as it is in the note. If the note changes while you're mid-experiment — you edited it in its own tab, restored it with Restore to last commit, ran a `git checkout` — the embed shows the note as it is now and says **Updated from note** for a few seconds: an experiment is only ever a scratch copy, and the note wins. Reset always goes back to the note as it is on disk right now. Start a second embed and the first one shuts up — one groove at a time. The Score button flips back to the grid when you press it again — and Reset does too, since the score would otherwise show what you just threw away. Here's the tresillo, ready to wreck:

```forge-rhythm

```
