---
description: "Tell which keys sit close: neighbours on the wheel share six of seven notes, and the key across the wheel shares only two."
---
Here is the real reason the circle of fifths exists: it is a ruler. It measures how far one key is from another, and so how well the notes and chords of one key sit with another. Close on the wheel, the keys get along. G sounds good with C. G sounds good with D. Look at the wheel: G sits right between them.

Every sector of the wheel is a major key, and every major key is seven notes. Here they are, clockwise from C:

| o'clock | key | its seven notes |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | C | C D E F G A B |
| 1 | G | G A B C D E F♯ |
| 2 | D | D E F♯ G A B C♯ |
| 3 | A | A B C♯ D E F♯ G♯ |
| 4 | E | E F♯ G♯ A B C♯ D♯ |
| 5 | B | B C♯ D♯ E F♯ G♯ A♯ |
| 6 | F♯ (or G♭) | F♯ G♯ A♯ B C♯ D♯ E♯ (G♭ spells it G♭ A♭ B♭ C♭ D♭ E♭ F) |
| 7 | D♭ | D♭ E♭ F G♭ A♭ B♭ C |
| 8 | A♭ | A♭ B♭ C D♭ E♭ F G |
| 9 | E♭ | E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C D |
| 10 | B♭ | B♭ C D E♭ F G A |
| 11 | F | F G A B♭ C D E |

Now put two rows side by side. Neighbours on the circle share six of their seven notes. That's why moving to one sounds smooth, like stepping into the next room instead of leaving the building. C and G share C D E G A B and differ by a single note: F in one, F♯ in the other.

Two steps apart and you share five notes. C and D share D E G A B. Three steps, four. The overlap shrinks with every step you take around the wheel, until you reach the opposite side. Six steps away, C and F♯ share only two notes: B, and F (the F♯ key spells it E♯). That's the longest jump you can make, and it sounds like it.

So the wheel is a map of distance. Close on the wheel means similar notes; far on the wheel means a different world. Keep that in your pocket: later, when you see which chords belong together, the same closeness comes back.

Try it: you get a key, and you find its two neighbours and the key directly across.
