## How it's computed

Take Pythagorean's twelve-fifth chain and shave a little off every single fifth instead of leaving them pure — a quarter of the *syntonic* comma each (about 5.4 cents), not the Pythagorean comma; the two are close cousins, about 21.5 versus 23.5 cents, and mixing them up is an easy way to sound like you don't actually know this stuff. Do that shaving twelve times and the major thirds — C to E among them — land almost exactly pure. Pythagorean chased perfect fifths and got ugly thirds. Meantone gives up a hair of fifth to buy a genuinely pure third instead.

## The tuning

Every fifth here is very slightly narrower than pure. You probably can't hear it on any single fifth. Wait until you hear what four of them add up to.

## Do the good

Four of those slightly-narrow fifths stack up to something Pythagorean never managed: a genuinely pure-sounding major third. C to E lands within a hair of the real 5:4 — no apology needed.

Below, C4, E4 and G4 start held. Play together in meantone, then flip to Pythagorean and play the same chord again. Hear how the meantone third settles, and the Pythagorean one doesn't? That's not a coincidence — that's the whole point of meantone.

## Do the bad

Here's the twist nobody mentions up front: meantone fixes the third by making the wolf even angrier. Temper eleven fifths, and the twelfth still has to eat the whole leftover — except now there's more of it to eat. Meantone's wolf lands sharp of pure by a wider margin than Pythagorean's ever managed flat. Cute trade, right? Fix one monster, feed the other.

Below, G♯3 and D♯4 start held — meantone's own wolf. Play together, then flip to Equal: worlds apart, same two keys. Worth it, for those pure thirds? Depends how many wolf keys you're willing to just... not use.

## History

Meantone took over keyboards from the late 15th century onward and stayed the default for two centuries — Renaissance and early Baroque composers wrote *for* it, not around it. The trick was simple: build fewer keys that actually get played, and just don't build the ugly ones. Some organs went further and added split keys, a black key sawn in two, so a player could sneak in an extra pitch class without retuning the whole instrument. It worked, right up until composers started wanting to modulate into keys nobody had built for. That's the itch [[well_temperament]] eventually scratched.
