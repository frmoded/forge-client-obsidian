Large-scale musical form didn't get designed top-down — it emerged bottom-up from dance music. Binary form (AB) is, at root, a Baroque dance-suite convention: two balanced halves, each repeated, because that's how a courtly dance actually worked in real time. Ternary form (ABA) extends the same instinct one step further — a middle section that goes somewhere else, then a return that makes the return itself feel earned. Sonata form, codified retroactively by 19th-century theorists describing what Haydn and Mozart had already been doing, is really an expanded, dramatized binary form: an exposition sets up a tonal conflict, a development intensifies it, a recapitulation resolves it — drama borrowed from a dance shape.

Today's verse/chorus/bridge pop form descends from the same sectional DNA — a repeating stable section (chorus ≈ refrain), a section that moves the story forward (verse ≈ strophe), and a contrasting departure (bridge ≈ the ABA middle section). The vocabulary changed; the underlying shape barely has.

**Listen for it:** notice the next time a song's bridge arrives — a key change, a new chord pattern, something that reads as "somewhere else" — that's ternary-form logic still doing its job, centuries later.
