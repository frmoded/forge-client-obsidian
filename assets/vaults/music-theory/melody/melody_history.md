Analyzing melody as a structure of MOTIVES — a small idea, developed, varied, recombined — owes an enormous debt to Beethoven scholarship. 19th-century theorists, Hugo Riemann among the most influential, built much of the vocabulary of motivic analysis specifically to explain how Beethoven built entire symphonic movements out of tiny cells, like the four famous notes that open the Fifth Symphony. Non-chord-tone vocabulary is older, tracing back to species counterpoint pedagogy (see [[counterpoint_history]]), where a "passing tone" was originally a rule about what a student was ALLOWED to write between two consonances, not yet a name for something composers used freely for color.

Phrase-and-subphrase language borrows openly from grammar — a period, a sentence, a comma — because 18th- and 19th-century theorists were reaching for exactly that analogy: music, like language, has units that complete a thought and units that merely pause. The analogy has aged well enough that it's still the literal vocabulary MT21C and every other modern textbook teaches.

**Listen for it:** hum the opening four notes of Beethoven's Fifth, then notice how often the rest of the movement echoes just that rhythm at different pitches — a motive being worked, not a coincidence.
