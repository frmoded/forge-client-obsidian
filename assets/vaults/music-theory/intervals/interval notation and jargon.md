You can count an interval. Now for the names: every distance has one, and musicians use them without blinking. Not sure how to count? That's [[what matters is intervals]].

A name has two parts: a **size** (2nd, 3rd, 4th and so on) and a **quality** (minor, major, perfect, diminished or augmented). Squash them together and you get a symbol: m3 for a minor third, M3 for a major third, P5 for a perfect fifth.

Here is the whole list, from 0 to 12 half steps:

- 0 half steps: unison (P1)
- 1: minor 2nd (m2)
- 2: major 2nd (M2)
- 3: minor 3rd (m3)
- 4: major 3rd (M3)
- 5: perfect 4th (P4)
- 6: tritone (d5 or A4)
- 7: perfect 5th (P5)
- 8: minor 6th (m6)
- 9: major 6th (M6)
- 10: minor 7th (m7)
- 11: major 7th (M7)
- 12: octave (P8)

You don't need all of them at once. The perfect fifth (7) and the minor and major thirds (3 and 4) are what the next chapters lean on hardest, so learn those first. [[thirds and fifths]] shows how they sit on the staff.

## Size counts letters, quality counts half steps

C up to E is a 3rd because it spans three letter names: C, D, E. Whether it's minor or major depends on the half steps: 3 (C up to E-flat) makes a minor 3rd, 4 (C up to E) makes a major 3rd. Same size, different quality.

## W and H: whole and half steps

A half step is the next key on the piano, black keys count. Call it H. A whole step is two half steps. Call it W. Scale recipes get written in those two letters, and major is W W H W W W H. Three half steps? That's a whole step and a half.

## Words you will meet

- **Half step** (also semitone): the next key on the piano, black keys included, or the next fret.
- **Whole step** (also tone): two half steps.
- **Ascending and descending**: going up or going down in pitch.
- **Melodic interval**: two notes played one after the other.
- **Harmonic interval**: two notes played together.
- **Unison**: two notes that are the same, 0 half steps apart.
- **Octave**: 12 half steps, the same note higher or lower.
- **Tritone**: 6 half steps, exactly halfway through the octave.
