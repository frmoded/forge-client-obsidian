Voice-leading rules didn't arrive as abstract aesthetics — they're a codification of what centuries of vocal polyphony found actually worked when real singers had to hold independent lines together with no conductor's help. Parallel fifths and octaves are avoided not because they're mathematically wrong but because they make two independent voices suddenly SOUND like one voice doubled — the opposite of the point of writing multiple voices at all. That aesthetic judgment hardens into explicit written rule across the Renaissance and Baroque, most rigorously in species counterpoint pedagogy (see [[counterpoint_history]]), and figured bass emerges alongside it as continuo players needed a fast, standardized shorthand to realize a bass line into full harmony in real time, during a performance — a professional's cheat-sheet, not a beginner's exercise.

By the time Bach is writing four-part chorales, these conventions have become so internalized they read as inevitable rather than chosen — which is exactly why they're still the default teaching material for the subject 300 years later.

**Listen for it:** play a slow four-voice chorale and try to follow just one inner voice all the way through — it moves by the smallest step available almost every time, which is voice leading working exactly as designed.
